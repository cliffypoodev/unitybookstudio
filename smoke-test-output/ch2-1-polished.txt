“Those payments aren’t for feed and coal; they are hush money.”

Margot did not raise her voice, but the quietness of her tone was sharper than glass on dry shale. She set the heavy, leather-bound book down upon the mahogany surface of the dining table—a book too grand for simple accounts, smelling faintly of mothballs, spilled bourbon, and old ambition. It was a ledger, bound in burgundy calfskin, its edges softened by decades of anxious hands, opened to a page filled with spidery script and columns of meticulous numbers.

Vivian sat across from her, the light catching the silver threads woven into her severe black dress, making her appear both faded and dangerously vibrant. She was already managing the slight tremor in her left hand—a habit she only indulged when cornered. Beside her, Chester leaned back in his chair, a man built of comfortable tweed and ingrained skepticism, nursing a glass of iced tea that beaded condensation around its crystal rim. He watched Margot with an amused, practiced air, as if this whole confrontation was merely another predictable squabble among cousins.

The room itself—the dining room at the edge of town in Harmony Creek—was suffocatingly beautiful: high ceilings draped with yellowed lace, walls paneled in dark oak polished to a deceptive sheen, and a window overlooking grounds that promised beauty but delivered only humid decay. It was a house built for grand pronouncements and kept alive by whispered secrets.

“They are support,” Vivian murmured, her gaze fixed on the ink of the ledger rather than Margot’s face. She smoothed her skirt with slow, deliberate movements, an act of self-soothing that barely masked the urgency in her eyes. “The upkeep for Mr. Davies, and Mrs. Pruitt’s linens, and Miss Elara’s schooling. Charity, all of it.”

Margot felt a familiar tightening in her chest—a mixture of affection for Vivian's unwavering facade and deep suspicion of the polished surface beneath it. “Charity doesn’t list full names next to every single tally mark, Viv. It lists needs. This,” Margot tapped the page with a fingernail worn into yellow,，“this lists individuals. Families.”

Vivian sighed, a dramatic, theatrical expulsion of air that seemed intended to exhaust the subject before it could fully form in their minds. She shifted slightly, her elbow brushing against the folded damask napkin on the side plate. With an almost ritualistic grace, she reached down and subtly lifted the corner of the open ledger page. Her fingers slipped into the spine, turning one page back—a careful, audible *scratch* of brittle paper against leather.

“The book is simply a record, Margot,” Vivian insisted, her voice gaining a defensive swell. “It’s been kept by three hands now, since your great-grandfather started accounting for everything from livestock feed to the opera tickets.” She gestured vaguely with her hand toward Chester, who merely inclined his head in agreement.

“And what does it account for, precisely?” Margot pressed, leaning forward until she was practically sharing the humid air of secrets with them both. “The estate’s assets? The annual harvest yields? Or the way one pays off a secret before it gets aired out like laundry on Sunday morning?”

Chester finally moved, reaching across the expanse of polished wood to place his hand flat upon the ledger, claiming ownership of its history. He wanted to sweep aside any implication that this book was doing more than merely counting money. “It’s just old bookkeeping, Margot. The family always kept everything accounted for, since times were harder and cash flow was a worry.”

“And what do you think is worth accounting for?” Margot challenged, her voice gaining an edge of heat. She moved swiftly, placing her hand over Chester's on the book itself, grounding herself in its weight—the heft of history. “The payments, Chester. They are too regular, too precise to be merely seasonal harvests.”

Vivian watched the exchange, her initial defensiveness softening into a watchful gravity.

It was Margot who saw it next. As she leaned closer, tracing the edge of the book with her index finger, her hand slipped between two thick pages near the binding—a space that should have been empty air. Her fingers caught on something small, stiff, and yielding: a receipt.

She pulled it out carefully, letting the brittle paper fall into her waiting palm. It was yellowed parchment, slightly foxed at the edges, bearing the unmistakable stamp of Mr. Jeremiah’s General Store down by the railroad tracks. But it wasn't the store name that caught her eye; it was the hand-written ledger entries beneath the date and amount.

Margot looked up slowly, raising the receipt like evidence in a courtroom drama. The script listed three families: Rivers, Thorne, and Dale. Three separate names, each connected to substantial sums of money—sums far exceeding what could be accounted for by simple support payments or seasonal necessities.

The silence that followed was not just absence of sound; it was a pressure, the kind that settles in the humid air before a summer storm, darkening everything beneath its shadow.

Chester’s easy amusement faded into surprise, his casual stance suddenly rigid. Vivian, who had been poised for denial, visibly flinched, her hand flying up to cover her mouth as if she had bitten back a gasp of guilt. She stared at the three names—Rivers, Thorne, and Dale—as if they were physical wounds opened by Margot’s gaze.

“They… those payments,” Vivian whispered finally, the denial dissolving into exhaustion. Her eyes dipped downward, focusing on the patterns in the tablecloth instead of their faces. “It wasn't just for upkeep. It was done to keep people quiet.”
