The cloying scent of polished cypress and dust-settled varnish clung to Margot’s clothes, a deep, sweet musk that spoke of decades of slow decay and forced grandeur. It was the smell of money trying too hard to be respectable—the humid breath of old Southern wealth preserved under glass and mothballed time.

Founder's Hall did not breathe; it merely absorbed. Its air was heavy, weighted down by generations of whispered triumphs and forgotten failures, a physical miasma that settled deep in Margot’s lungs with every measured step. Sunlight, filtered through the lace-curtained windows fell across the parquet floor in dusty shafts, illuminating motes of dust dancing over gilt trim and dark mahogany furniture. The room was built to impress meant for grand announcements and solemn endowments—a mausoleum dedicated to civic pride.

Margot walked straight past the reception desk where a uniformed boy stood, barely visible behind stacks of bound ledgers that smelled faintly of mildewed ink. Her eyes were fixed on the central monument: the Founder’s commemorative plaque, mounted at eye level near the main entrance. It was brass, polished to a deep, burnished gold, commemorating the completion date and listing the names—the pillars of Harmony Creek who had built this edifice.

She stopped before it, letting the cool metal settle against her fingertips, feeling the familiar grit beneath the shine. She pulled the folded receipt from her apron pocket—a flimsy, cream-colored slip of paper bearing the precise stamp of the Hall’s internal bookkeeping system. It was yellowed at the edges, singed slightly at the corners where she had worried it; the ink was black and permanent, a contrast to the patina of the surrounding brass.

The receipt represented something immediate,, and negotiable. The plaque represented permanence, historical mandate, the official version of the story. Margot unfolded her hand-written note from the receipt—a casual scribble in pencil that contradicted the formal ledger entry she was holding aloft. She matched the date stamped on the paper to the engraved dedication date beneath the founders’ names. They did not align perfectly; there was a slippage, a gap of weeks or months accounted for by no official record on the plaque itself.

Jebediah, who had been lounging against an overly ornate column near the donation registry—a man whose waistcoat strained across his considerable girth and whose smile never quite reached his pale eyes—straightened up, brushing imaginary dust from his trousers. He regarded Margot’s evidence with a practiced air of bored superiority.

“Ah, Miss Rivers,” he drawled, tipping his head back slightly as if observing a particularly dull spectacle. “The receipt. Always the receipt. Do you find much value in it?”

Margot met his gaze steadily, ignoring the faint scent of stale pipe tobacco and expensive cologne that clung to him like a second skin. She did not take her eyes off the plaque, instead allowing them to drift down to its base, where the brass mount connected to the stone pillar.

“I find value in what it contradicts,” Margot replied, keeping her voice level.

Jebediah chuckled, a dry, wheezing sound that lacked any true humor. “It’s just paper, Miss Rivers. A transaction record. We process enough transactions here to feed a small army of accountants.” He gestured dismissively with one hand, encompassing the entire display as if it were a pile of loose change unworthy of serious consideration. “A ceremonial donation, if I recall? One of those little tokens we collect for posterity?”

“It is more than that,” Margot insisted, her fingers tightening around the brittle paper. She was ready to fight this battle with every piece of evidence she possessed—the receipts, the schedules, the family gossip she had collected like rare specimens.

Before Jebediah could launch into a protracted dismissal of its merit, Silas shifted in his usual corner near the archival records, clearing his throat with the dry rasp of sandpaper on slate. Silas was the keeper of the hall’s physical history—the man who knew where everything stood and, more importantly, how things moved when no one was looking.

Silas stepped closer, moving around Jebediah's periphery like a shadow slipping past bright light. He did not address Margot directly; he addressed the plaque itself, his gaze clinically assessing the metalwork.

“Mr. Thorne,” Silas said, his voice low and gravelly, carrying the precise resonance of deep knowledge. “You make it sound as if we merely collected tokens.” He paused, letting the statement hang in the humid air, a challenge to Jebediah’s casual dismissal. Then he moved his hand, not toward the plaque itself, but downward, forcing everyone to look at the mounting point where the brass met the granite pedestal.

Silas ran a thumb along the seam beneath the main dedication panel. His fingers paused near the corner of the central cornerstone, dragging slightly. He repeated the motion once more, and then he drew his hand back, leaving a faint streak of dark grime on his skin.

“The plaque itself is magnificent,” Silas conceded, wiping his thumb against his trouser cuff. “But look closely at that joinery. The brass plate was installed, yes, but this stone mount… it’s newer.” He leaned in, forcing Margot and Jebediah to narrow their focus. His finger pointed precisely—not at the plaque itself, but into the minute gap where the outer border of the dedication panel met the supporting stone. “See those marks? They are staggered. Too evenly spaced for decades of settling dust.”

He tapped his finger lightly against the seam, confirming the shallow indentation. The mark was distinct: a faint, slightly oxidized crescent shape left by the screw head. Silas repeated the action on the opposite side, tracing another groove. “They suggest replacement,” he stated flatly. “Not just cleaning, but actual hardware points. Someone bolted this thing up with modern diligence.”

Jebediah’s polished façade cracked under the weight of physical proof. He shifted his weight, a subtle movement that betrayed alarm. “Nonsense, Silas. It’s humidity warping the stone.”

“The air in this hall is old enough to fossilize, Jeb,” Margot countered instantly, seizing on the thread of argument like an anchor. “And you do not get warped stone marks when you sweat through a silk waistcoat.” She glanced pointedly at Jebediah's collar.

Jebediah’s face flushed, a sudden rush of color climbing his neck and dusting his cheekbones. He took a step forward, his arms folding tightly across his chest, adopting the posture of defense—the protective stance of a man whose carefully curated narrative was fraying under scrutiny. He had to reclaim the moment, reassert the institutional authority that Margot’s simple paper ticket threatened.

He ignored Silas's precise observations about hardware and focused instead on the evidence in Margot's hand. “Miss Rivers,” he said, his voice tightening with forced paternalism. “You are digging too deeply into the woodwork here. This Hall has survived worse than a little paperwork dispute. You need to take your concerns—and this receipt—to Mr. Albright. He runs the accounts; he understands value.”

He reached out, not requesting, but commanding. His fingers brushed against her wrist as they moved toward the paper. “Hand it over. Let us file it away with the rest of the trivialities.”

Margot did not pull back. She allowed his hand to brush her skin, letting him believe he had victory, only to slide her finger underneath his grip and pull the receipt back into the curve of her own palm. His touch was possessive; his motive, desperate.

“I don’t want you to file it away,” Margot said softly, holding the paper out like a shield. “I want to understand what it means.” She looked at him, her gaze unwavering, dismissing the superficial grandeur of the Hall and focusing only on the man's strained composure.

She let the silence stretch until Jebediah seemed physically uncomfortable, his fingers twitching as if needing to grasp something solid—something that wasn’t historical fact or architectural detail. He merely stared at the receipt ready to deflect with a grand story about local charity drives or forgotten benefactors.

Instead of handing it over, Margot slipped her thumbnail beneath the corner crease of the paper. She worked gently, slowly, until she had peeled back the outer layer of the stub, revealing not just the faded adhesive strip, but something written on the back of the ledger-side copy—a note that was clearly added later, scrawled in a hurried hand with dark fountain pen ink, betraying none of the elegant copperplate script used elsewhere.

She tilted the paper for Silas and Jebediah to see, allowing the light to catch the uneven black lettering. It stood out against the cream background like an exposed nerve. The message was brief, sharp, and utterly undermining: *Keep the story clean.*

The words hung in the dusty sunlight between them—a private directive placed publicly on institutional record. It wasn't merely information; it was a warning, a confession, or perhaps a threat.

Margot let her hand fall away from the paper, letting the sheet drop slightly against her apron as if its weight had suddenly pulled all the air out of the room. She looked first at Silas, whose face had gone utterly still, his eyes widening subtly; then to Jebediah, whose forced smile finally vanished, leaving him looking merely exposed.

She didn't need them to interpret it for her. The words were a key turning in a lock she hadn't known was there. She held the note out, letting the truth—or at least, the threat of it—hang between Founder’s Hall and the three people standing within its embrace.

"You were never supposed to find her name."
