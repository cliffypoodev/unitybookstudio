The Old Mill

Margot had always believed that secrets, like good Southern soil, were deepest where they seemed most obvious; she assumed all truths lay buried just beneath the surface grime of a town’s daily life. That assumption—that history was simply accumulated dirt and gossip waiting to be scraped off—felt dangerously thin in the damp chill of the pre-dawn air. The old Harmony Creek Mill stood before her, not merely derelict, but slumped into its own legend, an edifice built more on rumor than on actual commerce.

She parked the car just off the overgrown gravel path that flanked the property line, the tires crunching against decades of accumulated shale and fallen oak leaves. The mill was massive, a skeleton of brick and clapboard struggling to hold itself upright against the relentless humidity of Alabama afternoons. Its stone foundation, stained dark with mineral runoff from Harmony Creek, seemed to absorb the meager light leaking into the valley. Vines, thick and desperate, choked the eaves, and kudzu draped over the upper window sills like green burial shrouds. It had been dormant for years—a monument to a time when cotton was king and everything revolved around the rhythmic clack of machinery.

Margot set her sensible leather briefcase down on the passenger seat. Inside rested the meticulous stack of ledgers, yellowed receipts, and Maude’s rambling accounts, all waiting to feed into the deep mechanisms of her investigation. She adjusted her gloves, brushing aside a spiderweb that snagged on her sleeve. The air around the mill tasted of wet earth, milled grain, and the metallic tang of neglect.

She walked toward the main entrance. The front portico sagged inward, bearing weight from time and moisture. Dust motes danced in the shafts of weak sunlight filtering through broken panes of glass—a slow, golden ballet of decay. Inside, the lobby was grand but hollowed out; polished hardwood floors bore deep gouges from forgotten wagon wheels. She moved past the counter where a cash register stood gathering dust like an inert beast and toward the back wing, following the direction Maude had indicated: the meeting room.

The hallway to the meeting rooms—a cluster of smaller annexes built into the side of the main structure—was darker, drier. The air here shifted; it carried less humidity, more the sharp, dry smell of old cedar and dried ink. Margot found the designated room easily enough. It was set slightly apart from the others, protected perhaps by its relative isolation. Unlike the rest of the mill complex which surrendered to rot, this small chamber stood remarkably intact.

It possessed a formal air that defied its location beside a creek and under the weight of decades. The walls were paneled in dark oak; heavy, moth-eaten drapes still hung from brass rods; and at the far end sat a massive, scarred mahogany table capable of hosting more than ten men—or women, or families, as Margot suspected.

She ran her fingers over the cool wood of the communion rail that separated the small reading area from the main space. It was undisturbed, almost preserved by ritual. She moved along the curved support beam running beneath the mantelpiece—a structural element meant to bear the weight of decorative molding, but which also offered a surface for time’s casual graffiti.

Margot paused. Her fingers traced the wood grain, finding nothing unusual at first. Then, her fingertips caught something rougher, slightly raised. She pressed closer, running her nails along the indentation until she found it: carved initials, worn smooth by handling and age.

She knelt, bringing herself close to the beam’s surface, letting the faint, sweet smell of aged wood fill her lungs. The carvings were deep, painstaking—not just initialisms, but whole names etched with careless permanence. She traced them one by one. There was *Arthur*, followed by a flourish for an ‘R’; beneath that, carved lower and slightly askew, appeared *Silas* and his ‘J.’ And finally, near the corner bracket, she identified *Jackson*.

Her heart gave a single, sharp kick against her ribs. She stood up slowly, dusting off her skirt. Three sets of names. Arthur R., Silas J., and Jackson—the three family names that had appeared on Maude’s receipts, the families whose tangled finances formed the core of the current mess she was trying to unravel.

She pulled her small camera from her bag—a heavy, reliable box-camera model favored by local photographers who still carried them as if they were magic—and began taking pictures. She framed the shot deliberately: the beam itself, showing its distressed state; then a close-up on the specific carving of Arthur R., ensuring the depth and style of the lettering registered; she moved to Silas J., capturing the unique slant of that etching; she repeated the process for Jackson.

The mechanical *snick* of the shutter was loud in the quiet room, cutting through the heavy air like a gunshot. She took several angles, rotating around the beam until the memory card (or film roll, depending on how deep she delved into the technique) was full of evidence. She made mental notes: the angle of the carving relative to the grain; the specific patina of the surrounding oak.

Satisfied with the documentation, Margot straightened up and began moving toward the exit, her mind already sorting through names and debts and potential betrayals. The mystery deepened with every carved name she confirmed. This wasn't just a meeting spot for local businessmen; it was a place where identities were made, affirmed, or perhaps buried.

She reached the doorway of the meeting room, turning to step out into the deeper gloom of the main hall when a sudden burst of light hit her back.

It was not the sun, nor the diffused glow of gas lamps inside the mill, but something external and powerful: headlights. Two beams, sweeping across the dusty windows behind her—the grand, arched windows that overlooked the creek bed and the overgrown lawn. They sliced through the pre-dawn gloom with startling intensity, brilliant cones of light that momentarily banished all the comforting grime of the setting.

Margot froze, one foot still planted in the meeting room threshold, the other hovering just outside. She turned slowly, her hand instinctively resting on the heavy brass door handle.

The beams did not sweep away; they paused, holding steady like twin spotlights aimed directly at the mill’s decaying façade and, more specifically, at Margot herself. They were too bright to identify the source immediately—they came from the road leading in, suggesting a vehicle that had arrived unexpectedly, or perhaps one that was waiting patiently.

She stood rigid, bathed in the sudden, overwhelming glare of approaching light, her hand still on the cold metal of the door handle.
