# Chapter 4: The Second Ledger

The lock gave with a heavy, rusty *thunk* that seemed to swallow the silence of the house whole. Margot pushed the door open, letting the cool, damp breath of the sub-level records room spill out into the porch air. She hung back in the doorway for a moment, letting her eyes adjust to the gloom—a perpetual twilight scented with mildewed paper and the cloying musk of damp earth. The gas lamps lining the hall upstairs were burning low, casting long, nervous shadows across the polished hardwood floors, but down here, everything was muted brown and green.

She clicked on the portable electric lantern she’d carried down, its single bulb throwing a yellow pool of light that barely stretched to illuminate the heavy oak desks pushed against the far wall. These desks—the ones belonging to Father Hemmings, who had been the primary record keeper for three generations of this crumbling estate—always seemed too large for the poor function they served now. They were monuments to administration and secrets.

Margot set her feet on the dusty floorboards, the sound muffled beneath a thick carpet of grit. She wasn't here for tax records or death certificates; those she had already cataloged. She was hunting the residue of bad memory, the things that didn’t fit neatly into manila folders labeled ‘Incident Report’ or ‘Client Billing.’

She moved first to the largest desk in the corner, its surface scarred by generations of ink spills and worried hands. The leather blotter mat felt sticky under her fingers. She ran her hand along the edge of the heavy mahogany drawer—the one marked *Financial Accounts: 1958–1962*—and pulled it open fully. Inside, stacks of brittle receipts lay like dried autumn leaves, their edges yellowed and fragile. Nothing new.

She wrestled with the second drawer, which housed the correspondence. These letters were mostly sentimental flotsam: wedding announcements, farm inventories, poems written by distant cousins. She sifted through them impatiently, her fingers brushing against the stiff vellum of old bond paper. *Trivial,* she thought, *all designed to keep the important things from being noticed.*

The third drawer was smaller, meant for personal files—the loose documentation of individual patients, family visits, and minor incidents. She pulled it open and began pulling out the contents systematically: intake forms, doctor’s notes, discharge summaries, all bundled with frayed twine. The weight of these documents always settled in her chest; they were not just paper, but evidence of lives altered—lives defined by diagnoses and caretakers' good intentions.

She worked methodically, running her knuckles along the vertical grain of the wood as she cleared out the visible clutter. She checked behind the brass pen cup, lifted the small porcelain inkwell, and shifted the stack of bound reports to check the underside. Nothing. Just dust, grit, and the faint, metallic tang of old iron.

Frustrated, Margot leaned her weight against the desk’s side panel, running her hand along the deep seam where the main body met the supporting structure. Her fingers snagged on something slight—a barely perceptible give in the wood grain near the bottom right corner. It wasn't flush.

She pushed harder, applying pressure just above the drawer track. There was a faint rasp, a sound like dry bone grinding against seasoned oak. She felt downward resistance and then, with a decisive shove, the entire section of paneling shifted inward by an inch or two.

Behind the false wood panel gaped a shallow cavity, lined crudely with black velvet that had faded to a dusty purple. It was not meant for documents; it seemed too small, too intimate. Nestled in the recess, resting on the velveteen cushion, was a single, leather-bound book.

It was unlike anything she had seen before. The cover was deep burgundy, worn smooth at the edges, and secured with braided gold thread. It wasn't cataloged anywhere; it felt separate from the system of record-keeping entirely. She reached for it, her fingers tracing the embossed lettering on the spine: *Maude’s Ledger*.

She pulled it free. The book was heavier than its size suggested, and when she opened it carefully, the scent that rose was stronger than the general mildew—it was the sharp, sweet smell of dried lavender mixed with old iron gall ink.

The pages were thick, creamy rag paper, yellowed to a butterscotch hue. They were not bound by the institutional stiffness of the other records; they felt personal, almost frantic in their arrangement. She settled onto an overturned crate nearby and began to read.

Maude Rivers’s handwriting was sweeping, dramatic, and skilled—a far cry from the clipped, official script used by the male doctors. The entries were not just dates and diagnoses; they were observations, judgments, and recollections filtered through a deeply personal lens of worry and suspicion.

*August 14th, 1962.*
*The air is too still today, darling. It hangs heavy off the creek like wet wool. Mr. Hemmings says everything is accounted for, but I saw his hands twitching when he spoke about the finances. His cuff links were askew. The kind of little slips that tell a story if you know where to look.*

Margot paused, her thumb resting on the ink-stained paper. *Everything* was not enough. She started reading faster, drinking in the rhythm of Maude’s paranoia and acuity.

The entries continued past the surface accounts—the missing funds, the questionable medication dosages, the late nights spent arguing with Father Hemmings over the disposition of the estate’s liquid assets. But then, the narrative deepened, moving beyond the immediate care facility drama into a wider geography.

*September 2nd, 1962.*
*It was at the old mill today. Reverend Davies insists that the truth is simply ‘poor bookkeeping.’ He smiled too wide for his face, and Mrs. Albright—her silk dress catching the light like spilled wine—nodded in agreement. But I saw Mr. Thorne’s ring flash on his finger when he spoke of the necessary adjustments to the property deeds. It wasn't just about the upkeep of Harmony Creek; it was about ownership, a claim staked deep into our little corner of Alabama soil.*

Margot leaned forward, gripping the book. The old mill. She knew of that place—a decaying brick structure on the outskirts of town, long abandoned since the decline of the railroad in the thirties. It had always been local folklore, a spot where people went to argue their fates or simply forget they were alive.

*October 19th, 1962.*
*They met after sunset, down by the river bend near the milling yard. Mr. Thorne was there, speaking quietly with Silas and even poor little Maggie—the one who never speaks unless she’s cornered. They spoke of the crisis not as a failure of care, but as an opportunity for consolidation. The money is needed to secure the land rights; the patients are merely the collateral.*

The entries were becoming less about accounting and more about conspiracy—a confluence of local power structures: property deeds, land claims, and the carefully managed lives of the residents. Maude wasn't just documenting care; she was writing a history of exploitation.

Margot’s heart began to beat a rhythm against her ribs that was too fast, too loud for the quiet basement. She flipped past several pages—references to lawyers, whispers about deeds, and an insistent focus on land ownership in the immediate vicinity of Harmony Creek.

She was reaching the final section of the book when she heard it.

It wasn't a sound from her own corner, or from the faint whirring hum of the electric light bulb above them. It was distinct, precise, and coming from the main hall upstairs—the creak of old wood settling under weight.

*Creeeeeeak.*

Margot froze, her breath catching in her throat. She gripped *Maude’s Ledger* so tightly her knuckles ached. The sound was too rhythmic to be natural settlement. It was deliberate. Someone shifting their weight, testing the floorboards.

She lifted her head, straining her ears against the sudden vacuum of sound that followed the creak. Silence returned, heavier and deeper than before. She waited, counting slow heartbeats in the absence of noise. Nothing. Just the faint drip of condensation somewhere deep within the foundation walls.

Had she imagined it? Fatigue? The sheer weight of the evidence she had found stacked up against her?

She stood abruptly, pushing herself away from the desk and the trove of secrets. Her boots scraped lightly on the grit-covered floorboards. She moved toward the narrow staircase leading upstairs, her hand trailing along the cold, dusty bannister rail.

The scent of lavender and old iron ink—Maude’s truth—was still heavy in her nostrils. She knew she couldn't leave this book here; it was too vital to be left out alone. But first, she had to face the source of the sound.
