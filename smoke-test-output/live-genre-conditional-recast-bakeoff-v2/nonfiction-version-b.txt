## Chapter 3: The Algorithm

David Hernandez wasn’t paid to be an investigator; he was paid to be an optimizer. He specialized in the kind of digital archaeology required when massive municipal bureaucracies decided that efficiency trumped intuition, and sometimes, basic fairness. His current assignment—optimizing the user interface for Milwaukee’s centralized hiring platform—had landed him deep inside the guts of CivicMetrics’ proprietary code repository. The system was enormous: a sprawling network designed to process roughly 50,000 applications annually across the city's twelve largest municipal employers, from fire department to public works.

For three weeks, David had been navigating APIs and SQL schemas, wrestling with the interface layer while trying to understand the logic powering the core decision-making engine. That engine was housed in a module named `candidate_scorer.py`. It was supposed to be simple: a weighted average of traditional metrics—years of experience, educational attainment, job-specific certifications. But as David scrolled through the weightings, he realized something unsettling. The score wasn't just about credentials.

The algorithm fed on five distinct input streams, four of which were far removed from standard HR practice. There was `Edu_Attainment_Score`, naturally; there was `Work_History_Years`; but then came the esoteric metrics: a variable for real-time social media sentiment analysis (`SocialNet_Activity`), a required field pulling credit bureau data (`Credit_Health_Index`), and, most disturbingly, a proprietary input labeled `Community_Stability_Index` (CSI).

The CSI wasn't just a simple count of local amenities. It was a complex, multi-variable calculation that weighted factors like average neighborhood income fluctuation, crime rate density, and proximity to major transit hubs. David leaned back from his monitor in the Delaney & Associates office, running a hand over the cool aluminum frame. The weight assigned to these non-traditional inputs—specifically the credit score and CSI—was staggering. They weren’t minor adjustments; they constituted nearly 40% of the total weighted scoring capacity.

He started by isolating the components that felt least like HR best practice. He ran a preliminary simulation, comparing applicants who were otherwise identical in their educational background and years of experience but whose application profiles varied only on those extraneous metrics. The results weren't merely skewed; they revealed systemic distress.

David focused his attention first on the zip code input embedded within the CSI calculation. He pulled up mapping data overlaid with scoring performance, creating a heat map of opportunity. The pattern was undeniable: applicants from nine specific zip codes south of Interstate 94—areas predominantly majority-Black or majority-Latino communities characterized by high turnover and complex economic strata—consistently scored lower.

He then correlated that spatial finding with the financial metrics. He pulled the raw data set, running a regression analysis to isolate the impact of the `Credit_Health_Index`. The correlation coefficient was extremely strong: worse credit scores were predictive of lower overall ranking, even when controlling for equivalent wages and job seniority listed on resumes.

The moment David ran his null test—the crucial comparison where he mathematically neutralized the influence of all non-traditional variables (zeroing out the CSI impact, removing the credit score weight)—the data spoke with brutal clarity. He reran the simulation using two control groups: a group of applicants from the nine specific south side zip codes versus a matched group from North Shore suburbs. When Education and Experience were held constant, the resulting performance gap was not minor. The algorithm’s predicted job success probability for the south-side candidates fell, on average, by 23 percentile points compared to their suburban counterparts.

(Hernandez, Internal Audit Report, 2026).

The data didn't lie about the mechanism of disadvantage. The algorithm wasn't explicitly coded to calculate "race" or "ethnicity"; it was blind to those labels in a pure sense. Instead, it had built an elaborate, highly effective proxy machine. It used zip codes and financial profiles—data points that are notoriously correlated with race and class—to perform the work of racial profiling without ever needing the explicit variable.

This wasn't just poor design; it was institutional capture. The City of Milwaukee, represented by its twelve powerful municipal employers, had contracted Delaney & Associates to implement a system designed for objective meritocracy. They paid premium rates for "AI-powered objectivity," and in doing so, they outsourced their judgment to an opaque black box developed by CivicMetrics—a firm whose profit model clearly relied on the predictive power of dubious peripheral metrics.

David realized that the problem wasn't a glitch; it was the embedded logic. The algorithm didn't just score candidates; it encoded history. It took the systemic economic disparities inherent in Milwaukee's geography and finance, quantified them into numerical weights, and then presented those historical inequities as objective predictions of future job performance.

To simply recommend that they 're-weight' the inputs would be treating a symptom. The heart of the matter lay in accepting that "community stability" was measurable by credit score, and that economic ZIP code was predictive of civic competence. David Hernandez had found not just an optimization problem, but a structural liability wrapped in Python code—a perfect machine for perpetuating class bias under the guise of cutting-edge data science.
