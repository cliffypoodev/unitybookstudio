The scent of magnolia oil and old dust coated Margot’s throat, thick enough to chew. It was a sweetness that had been pressed into the very wood grain of Harmony Creek—a heavy, cloying aroma suggesting too much history, too many secrets buried beneath the blooms.

Founder's Hall was not merely a room; it was an edifice built of memory and stubborn expectation. The air in the hall hung stagnant, filtered through massive Corinthian columns whose fluting seemed to weep with years of humid Southern languor. Sunlight, when it managed to pierce the grime-stained lancet windows did not illuminate so much as it struggled, falling across the polished mahogany floors in dusty, theatrical shafts.

Margot walked slowly, her sensible shoes clicking a brittle rhythm against the stone tile—a sound too loud, perhaps, for the solemn weight of the place. She clutched the small, yellowed receipt in her right hand, the edges softened by time and repeated handling. It was an innocuous piece of paper, listing dates, services rendered, and a single, suspiciously high monetary value paid out to the town’s most prominent benefactors.

She stopped before the central wall, where the Founders' plaque—a monumental affair of brass and carved oak—was mounted like a sacred promise. The plaque dedicated the hall, commemorating the original land grant and the establishment of Harmony Creek as a regional hub by the powerful families who had built it from nothing. It was meant to impress, solidifying their lineage into stone.

“You’re looking for something, Margot?” Silas prompted, his voice carrying the smooth, practiced resonance of a scholar who knew exactly how much he knows and how little he cares to admit. He stood slightly behind her left shoulder, perpetually poised, like a well-dressed vulture awaiting a meal.

Margot didn't look at him; she kept her gaze fixed on the plaque’s centerpiece—a deeply etched dedication panel listing names in sequential honor: *Mr. Jebediah Hawthorne, Mrs. Eleanor Vance, The Caldwell Line…*

“I’m looking for a match,” Margot said quietly, her voice sounding rougher than usual. “Something that belongs here, but doesn't quite fit.” She shifted the receipt in her fingers, letting the corner catch the meager light. “It feels like it should be etched somewhere.”

Jebediah, who had been leaning against a marble pillar nearby, ostensibly admiring his own reflection, pushed off with an audible sigh of theatrical exasperation. He was wearing a suit that always looked too expensive for this dusty town—a rich charcoal wool that spoke of Chicago tailoring and inherited money.

“Nonsense, girl,” Jebediah drawled, waving a dismissive hand toward the plaque. “It’s a receipt. A ledger stub. These old gentlemen are full of sentimental fluff. Everything here was donated, paid for, or given in kind. It's nothing but ceremonial donation proof.”

“But it has specifics doesn’t it?” Margot countered, her voice gaining an edge of steel that surprised even herself. “It details services rendered—a specific date, a considerable sum paid to the local treasury fund, and a vendor listed under ‘Historical Preservation.’ It feels… professional.” She held the receipt out slightly, letting the paper catch the light like a captive moth.

Silas stepped forward then, subtly positioning himself between Margot and Jebediah. He ran a finger along the lower edge of the plaque, his nails catching faintly on the oxidized brass. “Jebediah has a point, dear heart. It is merely documentation of good faith.” Silas paused, letting the silence stretch into something uncomfortable. “Though I must say, that particular plaque, the grand one you’re staring at—it was replaced last year wasn't it? By Council vote, if memory serves.”

Margot frowned. She knew the history of Founder's Hall better than most; she had spent her formative years within its comforting, dusty embrace. The plaque *had* always been there.

“I thought it was original,” Margot murmured, more to herself than to them.

Silas smiled, a network of fine lines crinkling around his eyes that never quite reached the warmth of genuine amusement. “The original one was brassier, darker, and featured a slightly different dedication grouping at the base. We moved everything, you know. To ‘modernize’ it.” He paused, allowing the implied gap in history to settle over them.

It was too much information, too many moving pieces, all converging on the simple transaction Margot held in her hand. She looked back at the plaque, forcing herself to ignore Jebediah's impatient tapping foot and Silas's knowing smirk.

She moved closer to the brass monument, ignoring the dust motes dancing in their wake, and peered at the metal itself. Her eyes traced the engraved lettering, but they kept snagging on something else: the structural reality of the piece.

“Silas,” Margot said, her voice dropping lower, drawing them into a conspiratorial whisper. “The plaque is beautiful, but look closer. Right near the bottom corner, where it meets the oak mantelpiece.” She gestured with her free hand. “There’s discoloration there. Not just age patina.”

Silas leaned in instantly, his scholarly mask slipping slightly to reveal genuine interest. He tilted his head, adjusting his spectacles. His gaze dropped to the base. Margot followed his focus, and they both saw it: two distinct shallow depressions in the mortar securing the plaque, too regular for natural settling. They were perfect points where screws had been driven—the faint, oxidized ghost marks of hardware that had once held a different object.

“They’re fresh,” Silas breathed out, almost reverently. “Or at least, recently installed.”

The trio fell into a charged silence, the humid air growing suddenly taut with unsaid accusations. The plaque wasn't just honoring history; it was built upon layers of *replacement*.

Jebediah, who had been observing the exchange like a hawk watching mice, finally snapped out of his musing state. He pushed himself off the pillar, taking two quick steps toward Margot, reaching out with surprising speed to pluck the incriminating piece of paper from her grasp.

“That’s enough, girl. Hand it over,” he demanded, attempting to fold the receipt into the pocket of his expensive trousers as if disposing of evidence. “It’s just a ledger stub. I said so.”

Margot did not flinch. She was ready for this; she had been running toward this confrontation since she entered the county line. Instead of letting him snatch it, she instinctively pulled her hand back, taking a step to her right—a small physical retreat that put her slightly outside the immediate reach of his grasping fingers.

She didn't release the receipt entirely. Instead, she shifted her grip, allowing her thumb to scrape lightly along the stub’s edge until she found the back side. The front was legible, detailing charges; the back had been hastily filled with something else. With a delicate movement, she peeled away the small corner of paper that contained the scrawl, lifting it slightly so they could read the cramped, looping script written in dark ink—a handwriting that spoke of urgency and deep paranoia.

Margot straightened up, holding the note up for them to see, her breath catching on the words. She didn’t need to speak them; the meaning settled over the room like a shroud.

“Keep the story clean.”

The three figures froze. The phrase hung in the air—a challenge thrown not at the plaque or the money, but directly at the careful edifice of their town's accepted narrative. They were all complicit in some degree: Silas knew too much about restorations; Jebediah was too eager to dismiss everything as profit; and the plaques themselves represented a polished version of truth.

Jebediah’s face, usually set in an expression of weary superiority, crumpled slightly. The easy arrogance drained away, leaving him looking merely like a man cornered by genuine exposure. He didn't argue with the note; he simply let it sink into the silence—the implication that everything they thought was true rested on a careful edit.

He took one last look at Margot, his eyes cold and suddenly predatory. His voice dropped, abandoning the performance of the wealthy patriarch for something far more dangerous—something personal. The air thickened around him, smelling now not just of magnolia oil, but of wet earth and old spilled blood.

"You were never supposed to find her name."
